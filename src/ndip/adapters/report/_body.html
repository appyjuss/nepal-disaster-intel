ERA5 sits on a grid far coarser than the valleys it is describing, and systematically under-reads orographic rain in the Himalaya. These figures establish a pattern; they are not an observation of what fell on Trishuli.

Why radar, not optical

every Sentinel-2 pass in the window

The instinctive design for this problem is an optical vegetation-index difference. In monsoon it fails silently: the pipeline runs, produces polygons, and the polygons are cloud edges. The last genuinely clear look at this ground was 12 August, fourteen days before the event.

How a detection earns its grade

The problem

Mountains fake change

Radar looks sideways, so steep terrain throws shadow and layover that read as change but are pure geometry. In a valley this deep it is the dominant source of false positives.

The control

Two look directions

Layover and shadow move when the look direction moves. Real ground change does not. Only a region seen from both an ascending and a descending pass is graded high — and two tracks sharing a look direction cannot corroborate each other.

The bar

Beyond mere detection

A single look direction can still convince, but only at roughly double the threshold that got the pixel detected. Setting that bar at the detection threshold made the grade automatic: 2142 of 2239 polygons scored medium and the grade meant nothing.

The floor

One hectare

Below about eleven 30-metre cells a region has no shape worth reporting. Smaller detections stay in the silver layer, flagged, so the threshold itself remains auditable.

What this does not tell you

  • Twelve days separate the images, in peak monsoon. Normal river-level change produces the same valley-floor darkening as flooding does. On radar alone these detections are not separable from seasonal variation — the single largest caveat on the headline result.
  • Change is not cause. Backscatter change detects that a surface differs, not why. Harvest, river migration, construction and flooding all register. Attribution stays human.
  • Not every track sees the whole area. Coverage is reported per track and should be read before trusting one; one of the three covers only a sixth of this area of interest.
  • A high detection is probably snow. Radar darkens sharply over wet snow, so a corroborated detection on a high steep face is more likely melt than a slope failure.
  • The terrain model predates the event. Copernicus DEM describes the slope that conditioned the failure, never the ground left behind by it.
  • No exposure analysis yet. Roads, bridges, buildings and population are the next layer. Nothing here says what was hit.
